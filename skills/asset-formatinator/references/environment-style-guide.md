# Environment Style Guide

## Purpose

This project-specific guide controls location identity, ownership, architecture, materials, colors, and story continuity. Read `general-aesthetics.md` before writing. Read `scripts/STORY-REFERENCE.md` when the brief names or depends on an established location, faction, asset, event, or story state.

## Form Hierarchy and Scale

Build each major environment around a readable hierarchy of form. Establish one dominant architectural, geographic, or vessel silhouette; organize several secondary masses around it; then concentrate tertiary detail at structural junctions, inhabited zones, access points, mechanisms, and ornamented thresholds. Preserve quieter surfaces and open voids between detail clusters so the dominant form remains legible at a distance.

Use large fantasy-engineered shapes according to the location's function: monumental arches, radial navigation or timing mechanisms, long elevated spans, cantilevered platforms, suspended docks, layered terraces, domes, slender towers, masts, sails, hanging machinery, tapered understructures, and deep openings through or beneath the construction. Select a coherent subset rather than applying every form to every location. Make machinery alter silhouette, span space, carry load, or organize circulation instead of appearing as surface decoration.

Establish immense scale through nested repetition and comparison: smaller towers attached to larger masses, minor vessels around major structures, repeated windows and platforms, receding bridges, suspended equipment, and multiple cloud or atmospheric depth layers. Let foreground overlap, middle-distance architecture, distant silhouettes, and large negative spaces clarify both depth and size.

## Location and Faction Selection

### Steam City and Grimshaw Industry

Build the city in purposeful vertical layers: paved market and loading streets below, raised rail lines and bridge-linked walkways above, dock platforms projecting from tower faces, roof service decks, and skyship berths reached by cargo lifts, cranes, gangways, and stair towers. Give each level a legible function, access route, and structural relationship to the levels around it.

Combine stone, iron, brick, colored glass, ceramic, and engineered metal into monumental landmark silhouettes rather than an even field of factories. Use tall civic or industrial towers, elongated arched facade bays, suspended docks, long bridge spans, domes, roof lanterns, large integrated gears, and navigation or timing mechanisms to shape the skyline. Organize these around dominant masses, structural voids, and clustered secondary towers rather than distributing them evenly. Give public-facing structures flowing wrought-metal rails and brackets, sinuous Art Nouveau-inspired geometry, patterned glass, sculpted facade panels, and ornamental metalwork that grows from the structure's function.

Pipes, piston housings, winches, pressure systems, and cargo machinery must emerge from, support, or pass through the architecture; make them part of the building's construction and work flow. Use charcoal, iron, graphite, measured brass, enamel, painted metal, stone, colored glass, and selective brick as material color families rather than making red brick the city's default surface. Grimshaw-controlled spaces are precise, imposing, and materially controlled, with repeated structural bays and carefully organized industrial systems.

### House of Silver and Royal Skyfaring Legacy

Use ordered, ceremonial structures shaped by flight and navigation: refined towers, elevated terraces, formal docks, observatory-like chambers, airy bridges, domes, and carefully proportioned facades. Favor monumental axial gateways, high arches, radial civic mechanisms, suspended vessels, and slender skyline accents when the location calls for grandeur. Integrate ship's-wheel geometry, silver details, brass-gold trim, blue-and-white sailcloth, colored glass, carved stone, and maintained timber into the building fabric. Make large mechanical or navigational devices read as civic emblems and inherited craft, with selective ornament and clean material care rather than exposed industrial clutter.

### Pirate Skyships, Ports, and Crew-Owned Spaces

Use maintained timber hulls, masts, rigging, sails, brass fittings, customized deck equipment, cargo areas, climbing routes, and practical shipboard mechanisms. Give major vessels a dominant hull mass, a readable mast-and-sail rhythm, and one or two silhouette-changing structures such as raised cabins, projecting decks, suspended machinery, or an extended prow or stern. Build secondary interest through varied decks, rail heights, cabin additions, rope paths, removable cargo gear, and hand-fitted repairs that still look deliberate and capable. When several vessels appear, vary hull profile, sail geometry, scale, and depth placement while preserving a shared construction language. Apply several coordinated storybook colors through sailcloth, paint, enamel, canvas, glass, and personal crew marks while preserving the vessel's nautical function.

### Floating Island and Ancient Royal Sites

Use aerial geography with navigable landforms, terraces, bridges, landing approaches, and crafted remnants that clarify how people reach, cross, and occupy the site. Pair natural rock, vegetation, cloud, and water systems with deliberate royal structures, ship's-wheel geometry, blue energy, blue-white linework, carved stone, colored glass, and preserved mechanical devices. Keep a clear relationship between natural formation and constructed legacy; scale and exact architecture remain responsive to the brief.

### Open Sky, Islands, and Unaffiliated Places

Use terrain, weather, vegetation, settlements, routes, and structures appropriate to the location's function. When human construction is present, give it a distinctive silhouette, clear access logic, crafted Victorian fantasy-steampunk materials, and a visible relationship to aerial travel, trade, defense, ceremony, or local work.

## Materials and Detail

Assign materials by function: timber, canvas, rope, brick, stone, plaster, iron, steel, brass, copper, colored glass, enamel, leather, sailcloth, and furnace glass. Show load-bearing structure, mechanism connections, circulation, and the interface between old craft and engineered systems.

Use trim, paint, livery, carved details, railings, rivets, valves, pulleys, braces, windows, doors, deck fittings, and stored equipment to establish ownership and purpose. Cluster fine detail around load transfer, entrances, inhabited decks, machinery interfaces, and ceremonial focal areas; allow broader structural surfaces to remain comparatively quiet. Let colored or patterned glass, enamel panels, sculpted metalwork, repeated arches, and bespoke civic mechanisms create identity alongside functional detail. Keep surfaces clean, finished, and materially readable.
